How to Raise Your Freelance Rates: The Email Script + Timing Formula

By Gregory Patterson · August 2, 2026 · 6 min read

Most freelancers raise their rates the way they ask for a favor: nervously, at a random moment, apologizing mid-sentence. It works about as well as it sounds.

The fix is to make rate increases boring. Put them on a schedule, use a formula, send the same email every time. Raise on a schedule, not on courage.

When to raise: the three signals

  • You're fully booked. If you're turning away work, your price is below what the market will pay. Full capacity is the clearest signal there is.
  • You keep winning proposals without negotiation. If every prospect says yes immediately, you were the cheapest option in the pile.
  • It's been 12+ months at the same rate. Your costs went up. Your skills went up. Your rate stayed flat — which means it effectively went down.

A simple cadence: every 3 months, or every 2 new clients, whichever comes first, raise 10–15%. Small, regular increases beat one heroic jump every three years.

The formula: notice + reason + deadline

A rate increase that lands has three parts:

  1. Notice — 6–8 weeks ahead. Ambushing a client with a new rate next Monday forces a yes/no under pressure. Notice lets them budget.
  2. Reason — one honest sentence: demand, results, or expanded scope. You don't need to over-justify; you need to not sound arbitrary.
  3. Deadline — the date the current rate stops being available. This is the part that makes people act instead of "getting back to you."

The email, verbatim

"Starting [date], my rate for [service] moves from $X to $Y. Anything we kick off before [date] is billed at the current rate — so if you've been thinking about [project], now's a good moment."

Notice what it doesn't contain: an apology, a justification essay, or a question mark. It's a statement with a built-in incentive to book now. Most clients simply accept — and some will accelerate a project they'd been sitting on, which means the raise email itself can generate work.

The three responses you'll get

1. Acceptance (most common). "Makes sense, thanks for the heads-up." Reply with gratitude and move on. This is roughly 80% of cases when you give proper notice.

2. Negotiation. "Can we stay at the old rate?" Decide your rule before you send: either hold ("The new rate reflects the scope and results we've built together — I'm confident it'll continue to pay for itself") or offer a transition ("Current rate holds for projects started before [date]"). What you don't do is fold instantly — an instant retreat teaches them every future email is negotiable.

3. Departure. Occasionally a client leaves. Here's the math nobody does: if the bottom 10% of your clients by value leave, and everyone else pays 15% more, your income goes up while your workload goes down. Losing your cheapest client is a promotion.

New clients vs. existing clients

New clients get the new rate immediately, with no announcement needed — they never knew the old one. Existing clients get the 6–8 week notice above. Never tell a new client "I used to charge less"; anchor every new relationship at your current price.

Make it a habit, not an event

Put the next increase in your calendar right now — a date three months out. When it arrives, copy the email, fill in the brackets, send. The whole ritual takes twenty minutes, four times a year, and it compounds into a career's worth of income difference.
