Late Payment? The 3-Strike Invoice Reminder Sequence for Freelancers

By Gregory Patterson · August 2, 2026 · 5 min read

The invoice was due yesterday. Nothing. Your brain offers two bad options: say nothing and stew, or send something furious and burn the relationship.

There's a third option: a sequence. Three pre-written emails, sent on a fixed schedule, that escalate firmness without ever escalating rudeness. Most invoices get paid at strike one — but you need strikes two and three written before you're angry, because angry-you writes bad emails.

The principle: assume kindness, then process, then paper

  • Day 1: assume kindness. 90% of the time, the invoice slipped — a missed notification, an approver on vacation, an AP batch that runs Fridays.
  • Day 7: assume process. It's stuck somewhere in their system. Make it easy to unstick.
  • Day 14: switch to paper. The agreement you both signed now does the talking.

Strike 1 — Day 1: the friendly flag

"Hi [Name], invoice #[X] for [$Y] was due yesterday — just flagging in case it got lost in the shuffle. Payment link here for convenience: [link]. Thanks!"

Light, short, zero accusation. "Lost in the shuffle" gives them the face-saving excuse — and most of the time it's literally true. The payment link matters: every extra click between reminder and payment costs you days.

Strike 2 — Day 7: the direct ask

"Hi [Name], following up on invoice #[X] for [$Y], now 7 days past due. Could you confirm when payment is scheduled? If there's a hold-up on your side (approvals, AP process), let me know what you need from me — happy to resend or reformat. Payment link: [link]."

Still warm, but now you're asking a direct question: when. Notice the offer to help — resending, reformatting, adding a PO number. Real payment delays are usually process problems, and solving the process problem is faster than moral pressure.

Strike 3 — Day 14: invoke the agreement

"Hi [Name], invoice #[X] for [$Y] is now 14 days past due. Per our agreement ([section], signed [date]), late payments accrue [X]% per [period] and work on [project/retainer] pauses while invoices are outstanding. I'd much rather keep things moving — can you confirm a payment date this week? Payment link: [link]."

No anger, no threats — just the document. This is why the late-payment clause exists: it lets the contract be the bad guy while you stay professional. "Work pauses" is the sentence that gets attention; use it only if your agreement actually says it.

Prevention beats collection

  • Deposits before work starts — 30–50% up front filters out almost every chronic late-payer before they cost you anything.
  • Late-fee clause in every agreement — even if you never enforce it, its presence changes payment priority.
  • Net-7 or Net-14, not Net-30 — shorter terms mean the reminder sequence starts while the work is still fresh in their mind.
  • Auto-reminders in your invoicing tool — strike 1 should go out even when you're too busy to notice the date.

Getting paid isn't a confrontation. It's a schedule with three sentences on it.
