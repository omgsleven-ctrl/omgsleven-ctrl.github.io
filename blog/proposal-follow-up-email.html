The Proposal Follow-Up Email That Revives Dead Deals

By Gregory Patterson · August 2, 2026 · 5 min read

You sent the proposal three days ago. Silence. Your brain starts writing the story: they hated the price, they picked someone else, you said something wrong.

Here's what's usually true: they got busy. Your proposal is sitting in an inbox between a shipping delay and a staffing problem. The deal isn't dead — it's buried. And buried deals are recoverable with one email, sent on the right days, with the right tone.

Why most follow-ups fail

The instinctive follow-up sounds like this: "Just checking in!! Did you get my proposal?? Would love to hear your thoughts!!!" Three exclamation points deep, it radiates anxiety. Worse, it creates social debt — the client now owes you an apology for not replying, which makes replying even harder. Every day they don't answer, the debt grows, and the easiest escape is to never answer at all.

A good follow-up does the opposite: it removes the debt.

The email, verbatim

"Just floating this back up in case it got buried. If timing's off, no problem at all. Either way, wishing you luck with [goal]."

Three sentences, each doing a job:

  • "Floating this back up" — you're not asking why they didn't reply. You're assuming inbox physics, not rejection. This keeps their dignity intact.
  • "No problem at all" — the magic line. It explicitly cancels the social debt. Replying now costs them nothing, even to say no.
  • "Wishing you luck with [goal]" — you end on their objective, not your invoice. It signals you're paying attention to their business, which is the whole reason to hire you.

Note the exit you give them: "if timing's off." Paradoxically, offering a graceful no is what produces yeses — because the clients who are still interested feel zero pressure, and the ones who aren't will often tell you, which frees you to spend energy elsewhere.

The timing schedule

  1. Day 3 — the float-up email above. Most replies arrive here.
  2. Day 7 — add value, don't repeat yourself: "One thing I didn't include in the proposal — a quick thought on [specific detail of their project]: [one genuinely useful sentence]. Full proposal still here whenever you're ready."
  3. Day 14 — the close-out: "I'll assume timing's not right and close this out on my end — if it comes back to life later, you know where to find me. Good luck with [goal]." Close-outs get a surprising number of same-day replies; a little honest scarcity works better than endless availability.

After day 14, stop. Three touches, then silence. Persistence is professional; pestering is memorable for the wrong reason.

Prevention: make the proposal easier to answer

The best follow-up is the one you don't need. Two habits cut ghosting dramatically:

  • Set the expectation inside the proposal: end with "I'll check in on [day] if I haven't heard — no need to reply before then." You've pre-authorized your own follow-up, so it never feels like chasing.
  • Answer the three skim questions above the fold: what do I get, when, what does it cost. A proposal that's easy to evaluate is a proposal that gets evaluated.

Ghosting isn't rejection. It's a queue position. Your job is to keep the deal easy to say yes to — and easy to come back to.
